It uses an additive model to predict future trends, seasonality, and holiday effects while handling missing data, outliers, and changes in trends effectively. Prophet is designed to work well with time series data, even with irregular observations or noisy data.</p><p>In this project, Prophet is used to forecast SPY’s monthly closing prices by modelling its trends and seasonal patterns. Prophet’s ability to automatically detect and account for trend changes and seasonality makes it a good choice for capturing the overall trajectory of SPY’s historical data.</p><h3>Prophet Architecture</h3><p>Prophet models time series data through an additive framework, breaking it into three main components: trend, seasonality, and holiday/event effects. These components are combined with a noise term to form the full model.</p><p>Prophet decomposes a time series into three key components to model trends, seasonality, and holiday effects. These components work together to provide interpretable and robust forecasts. We made reference to the Prophet paper by Taylor, S.J., &amp; Letham, B. (2017).</p><h3>1. Trend Component — $g(t)$</h3><ul><li><strong>Purpose</strong>: Captures long-term growth or decline trends in the time series data.</li><li><strong>Formula </strong>for <strong>piecewise linear growth with changepoints</strong>:</li></ul>

$$
g(t) = \left(k + \mathbf{a}(t)^\top\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)t
     + \left(m + \mathbf{a}(t)^\top\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)
$$

<ul><li><strong>Variables:</strong></li><li>$k$: Growth rate before any changepoints.</li><li>$m$: Offset parameter (intercept).</li><li>$\boldsymbol{\delta}$: Rate adjustments at changepoints.</li><li>$\boldsymbol{\gamma}$: Adjustments ensuring continuity at changepoints.</li><li>$\mathbf{a}(t)$: Binary vector indicating whether $t$ has passed each changepoint.</li></ul><ul><li>For logistic growth with changepoints:</li></ul>

$$
g(t) = \frac{C(t)}{1 + \exp\left(-\left(k + \mathbf{a}(t)^\top\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)t - \left(m + \mathbf{a}(t)^\top\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)\right)}
$$

<ul><li><strong>Additional Variables</strong>:</li><li>$C(t)$: Time-varying carrying capacity (growth ceiling).</li><li>For our SPY data, $g(t)$ will use the linear trend. We will explain why later.</li></ul><h4>Change Point Definition</h4><p>A <strong>change point</strong> in time series analysis refers to a specific point in time where the statistical properties of the time series data undergo a significant shift. In the context of Prophet, change points are used to:</p><ul><li>Represent abrupt changes in the trend of the time series data.</li><li>Indicate points where the growth rate of the data either accelerates, decelerates, or changes direction.</li></ul><h3>2. Seasonality Component — $s(t)$</h3><ul><li>Purpose: Models recurring periodic patterns in the data (e.g., yearly, weekly cycles).</li><li>Formula</li></ul>

$$
s(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[a_n\cos\left(\frac{2\pi nt}{T}\right) + b_n\sin\left(\frac{2\pi nt}{T}\right)\right]
$$

<ul><li><strong>Variables:</strong></li><li>$N$: Number of Fourier terms.</li><li>$T$: Period of the cycle (e.g., 365.25 days for yearly seasonality).</li><li>$a_n,b_n$: Coefficients capturing amplitude and phase.</li></ul><ul><li>SPY Data Context: Seasonality plays a minor role in long-term SPY forecasting but can capture weekly trading cycles.</li></ul><h3>3. Holiday and Event Component — $h(t)$</h3><ul><li><strong>Purpose</strong>: Models the impact of specific events (e.g., holidays, earnings reports).</li><li><strong>Implementation</strong>: Custom regressors are used to capture these effects.</li><li><strong>Formula:</strong></li></ul>

$$
h(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{I}(t)\right]
$$

<ul><li>$\mathbf{I}(t)$: A vector of zeros and ones indicating which holidays or events are active at time $t$.</li><li>$\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\top}$: Learned vector of coefficients related to each holiday.</li></ul><ul><li><strong>SPY Data Context</strong>:<br>Market holidays (e.g., Thanksgiving) and economic events (e.g., Federal Reserve Announcements) can influence SPY prices in the short term but are less impactful over longer horizons</li></ul><h3>Full Model Equation</h3><p>The final Prophet model is:</p>

$$
y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + \varepsilon_t
$$

<ul><li>$y(t)$: Observed value at time $t$.</li><li>$\varepsilon_t$: Random noise not captured by the model components.</li></ul><h4>Why Linear Trend?</h4><p>The choice of a <strong>linear trend</strong> over logistic growth in modelling SPY data is based on a simple consideration.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-4-prophet-and-lstm/C3Z7HfrcSyn9bNMMEteYVg.png" /><figcaption>Figure 14: Visualisation of Logistic Growth</figcaption></figure><p><strong>Unbounded Growth:</strong> SPY price data does not have a fixed upper limit or saturation point, as seen in logistic growth. Financial markets are influenced by numerous factors like inflation, economic growth, and innovation, which allow prices to grow indefinitely.</p><ul><li>In contrast, logistic growth assumes a <strong>carrying capacity</strong> $C$, representing a maximum level beyond which growth cannot proceed. This assumption is unsuitable for SPY data.</li></ul><h3>Why Log Returns Are Not Feasible for Prophet</h3><h4>Challenges with Log Returns</h4><ol><li><strong>Exponential Price Conversion</strong>: Prophet predicts log returns, which must be converted back to prices iteratively using:</li></ol>

$$
P_t = P_{t-1} \times e^{r_t}
$$

<p><em>Small prediction errors in log returns compound exponentially, leading to unrealistic price forecasts.</em></p><p>2. <strong>Trend Extrapolation</strong>:</p><ul><li>Historical patterns of high volatility or prolonged trends are extrapolated into the future, exaggerating long-term predictions.</li><li><strong>What It Means</strong>: Historical patterns of <strong>high volatility</strong> or <strong>prolonged trends</strong> (such as a continuous upward or downward movement in the data) are used by Prophet to make future predictions.</li><li><strong>Why This Is an Issue</strong>: If the log returns in the past show consistent growth (e.g., during a bull market), Prophet assumes this trend will persist into the future. When these extrapolated log returns are converted back to prices, the result can lead to <strong>unrealistic exponential growth or decline</strong> in prices, making the predictions less reliable over long horizons.</li></ul><h3>Why This is Problematic</h3><h4>Problem 1:</h4><p>Log returns $r(t)$ describe the proportional change in price:</p>

$$
P(t) = P(0) \times e^{r(t)}
$$

<p>Over multiple periods, log returns accumulate multiplicatively:</p>

$$
P(t) = P(0) \times e^{r_1} \times e^{r_2} \times \cdots \times e^{r_t}
$$

<p>Prophet cannot naturally handle this compounding effect because it adds the components linearly, which oversimplifies the true dynamics.</p><h4>Problem 2:</h4><p>Additive Formulation Leads to Linear Growth:</p><p>Using Prophet’s trend component $g(t)$, for example:</p>

$$
g(t) = \left(k + \mathbf{a}(t)^\top\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)t
     + \left(m + \mathbf{a}(t)^\top\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)
$$

<p>Here, $k$ is the base growth rate, and $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ adjusts the rate at changepoints. This formulation assumes linear growth (piecewise or otherwise), which is incompatible with the exponential growth behaviour of log returns.</p><h4>Problem 3</h4><ol><li><strong>Distortion of Trends Over Time</strong></li></ol><p>When log returns are modelled additively, the resulting predictions are a linear sum:</p>

$$
\hat{r}(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t)
$$

<p>Converting this back to prices via exponentiation introduces systematic bias because the additive nature cannot replicate the compounding process accurately:</p>

$$
P(t) = P(0) \times e^{\hat{r}(t)}
$$

<p>Errors in fitted $r(t)$ propagate exponentially, leading to increasingly unreliable price predictions.</p><h3>Why This Matters</h3><p>Using log returns with Prophet introduces a <strong>mismatch in modelling dynamics</strong>:</p><ul><li>Log returns depend on multiplicative compounding, but Prophet assumes additive relationships.</li><li>This results in <strong>distorted long-term forecasts</strong> when log returns are converted back to prices.</li></ul><p>Switching to prices avoids these issues by aligning the data structure with Prophet’s additive model, leading to more intuitive and accurate predictions.</p><h3>Prophet Model Workflow</h3><h3>1. Pre-processing</h3><ul><li>Renamed columns to Prophet’s format (ds for dates, y for target variable).</li><li>Prophet requires columns named ds (date) and y (target variable) to standardize the input format. This ensures compatibility across different datasets and eliminates the need for users to define column mappings manually.</li><li>The ds column is used to identify the time dimension of the data, while the y column represents the observed values Prophet forecasts.</li></ul><h3>2. Train-Test Split</h3><p>To evaluate the model’s performance, the dataset was split into:</p><ul><li><strong>Training Set (80%)</strong>: Used to train the model.</li><li><strong>Testing Set (20%)</strong>: Evaluate model performance on unseen data.</li></ul><p>The split maintained the temporal sequence to preserve the time-series nature of the data.</p><h3>3. Cross-Validation</h3><p>Prophet’s built-in cross-validation function (cross_validation) was employed on the training set to simulate how the model performs on unseen data.</p><p><strong>Process</strong>:</p><ul><li>The model was repeatedly retrained on progressively larger subsets of the training data, predicting the next horizon (365 days) for each fold.</li><li>Performance metrics were calculated for each fold.</li><li><strong>Percentage of Dataset Used</strong>:</li><li>In the first fold, approximately <strong>15%</strong> of the training dataset was used for validation.</li><li>With each fold, the percentage increased as the training window expanded.</li></ul><p><strong>Performance Metrics</strong>:</p><ul><li><strong>Mean Squared Error (MSE)</strong>.</li><li><strong>Coverage</strong>: Percentage of actual values within confidence intervals.</li></ul><h3>4. Forecasting</h3><h3>Using the Full Dataset</h3><p>A new Prophet model was fitted using the entire dataset to forecast 20 years into the future. However, its estimates are more bearish compared to the returns benchmark, reflecting a cautious approach to forecasting long-term growth. This conservatism stems from Prophet’s focus on capturing seasonality, trends, and holidays rather than extrapolating aggressive growth rates.</p><p>Despite its cautious predictions, Prophet remains valuable as it aligns closely with realistic market scenarios, avoiding overestimation while maintaining consistency with historical performance trends. Its ability to handle non-linear trends and incorporate external factors further enhances its robustness, making it a reliable tool for financial forecasting when paired with other models.</p><h3>Model — LSTM</h3><p>LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) is a type of RNN designed to effectively learn from sequential data by addressing the vanishing gradient problem in traditional RNNs. Unlike standard RNNs, LSTMs use a memory cell and gates (input, forget, and output) to regulate the flow of information, allowing the network to retain long-term dependencies. This makes LSTMs ideal for tasks like time series forecasting, where understanding temporal relationships is crucial.</p><p>LSTM networks are utilized to forecast SPY’s future returns by capturing long-term dependencies in the time series data. LSTMs are well-suited for this task because they can effectively model the sequential patterns in SPY’s while mitigating issues like the vanishing gradient problem. By leveraging the network’s memory and gating mechanisms, the model aims to generate reasonable predictions of SPY’s long-term trends.</p><h3>The LSTM Architecture</h3><p>The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network is designed to learn both short-term and long-term dependencies in sequential data, such as SPY’s historical log returns. Its architecture consists of a <strong>memory cell</strong> and three gates that regulate the flow of information at each time step:</p><h3>Internal Mechanisms</h3><ol><li><strong>Forget Gate ($F_t$)</strong>: Decides which information to discard from the previous memory state using a sigmoid activation function:</li><li><strong>Input Gate ($I_t$)</strong>: Determines which new information to add to the memory cell by combining a sigmoid function (to decide what to update) and a tanh function (to decide how much to update)</li><li><strong>Output Gate ($O_t$)</strong>: Controls the output by filtering the updated memory state ($C_t$) through a tanh function and scaling it with a sigmoid function:</li></ol><h3>Inputs and Outputs</h3><p>The <strong>memory cell</strong> ($C_t$) integrates the forget and input gates to store information over time:</p><p>Finally, the <strong>hidden state</strong> ($H_t$) is derived from the memory cell and the output gate:</p><p>We can have the following interpretation of the hidden state and the cell states.</p><ul><li><strong>Hidden State</strong>: Short-term memory, updated at each step, summarizes the current moment.</li><li><strong>Cell State</strong>: Long-term memory, carries information across the sequence.</li></ul><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-4-prophet-and-lstm/BZhP05QEtaEdJ1dS-7rKfQ.png" /><figcaption>Figure 16: LSTM Model Architecture, Credits : <a href="https://d2l.ai/chapter_recurrent-modern/lstm.html">https://d2l.ai/chapter_recurrent-modern/lstm.html</a></figcaption></figure><h3>How the LSTM Learns SPY Data</h3><p>The LSTM model is applied to SPY’s historical log returns to predict future returns by processing sequences of data and capturing temporal dependencies. The architecture described above ensures the model can focus on both short-term fluctuations and long-term trends.</p><p>The dataset consists of 373 monthly data points representing SPY’s log returns. To train the LSTM, we split the data into overlapping sequences, each containing 120 consecutive time steps $X_1$, $X_2$, …, $X_{120}$.</p><p>The choice of a sequence length of 120 for this LSTM Model due to the need to capture meaningful temporal patterns in SPY’s monthly log returns while balancing computational efficiency.</p><p>These sequences are fed into the LSTM as input, acting as sliding windows over the data.</p><p>For example:</p><ul><li>The first sequence contains data points $X_1$ to $X_{120}$ ,</li><li>The second sequence contains $X_2$ to $X_{121}$, and so on.</li></ul><p>Each sequence is paired with a target value y, representing the log return immediately following the sequence $X_{121}$ , $X_{122}$ etc.). This setup uses a sliding window approach, where the model leverages the past 120 log returns in the sequence to predict the next log return. By processing overlapping windows, the LSTM learns how historical patterns influence future returns.</p><h3>Processing Through Layers</h3><p>The model is configured with two LSTM layers (num_layers = 2).</p><p>The two-layer structure enables the model to learn at different levels of abstraction:</p><ul><li>The <strong>first layer</strong> captures simple temporal dependencies, such as month to month fluctuations.</li><li>The <strong>second layer</strong> identifies more abstract and long-term patterns, such as trends over multiple months.</li></ul><p>Two layers strike a balance between model complexity and computational efficiency, avoiding overfitting while capturing meaningful patterns in the SPY data.</p><p><strong>First Layer</strong>:</p><ul><li>The <strong>first LSTM layer</strong> processes the input sequence one time step at a time. For each time step t, it passes the values of the sequence through all of the gates and outputs the hidden states and the cell states.</li><li>The first layer outputs a sequence of hidden states $H_1$, $H_2$, …, $H_{120}$, summarizing the learned information at each time step.</li></ul><p><strong>Second Layer</strong>:</p><ul><li>The <strong>second LSTM layer</strong> takes the hidden states from the first layer $H_1$, $H_2$, …, $H_{120}$ as input and processes them similarly.</li><li>This layer refines the information from the first layer, extracting higher-level features and long-term dependencies.</li></ul><ol><li><strong>Final Output</strong>:</li></ol><ul><li>The last hidden state ($H_{120}$) of the second layer represents the most refined understanding of the sequence and is used to predict the target value (y).</li></ul><p>Only $H_{120}$, the hidden state from the final time step, is passed to the next layer because it represents a filtered and processed summary of the sequence, capturing both short-term and long-term dependencies. In contrast, $C_{120}$, the cell state, is designed to function as layer-specific long-term memory, internal to the current layer. Each layer independently manages its own cell state to learn patterns at its level of abstraction, ensuring modularity and preventing entanglement between layers.</p><p>In total, there will be 240 cells processing the monthly SPY log returns, with 120 cells dedicated to each sequence length across 2 layers.</p><h3>Why This Works for SPY Data</h3><p>The LSTM’s architecture is ideal for time series data like SPY’s log returns:</p><ul><li>The <strong>forget gate</strong> enables the model to filter out irrelevant historical information.</li><li>The <strong>input gate</strong> allows the model to focus on relevant new information.</li><li>The <strong>memory cell</strong> retains long-term patterns, such as market trends and economic cycles.</li><li>The <strong>two-layer structure</strong> allows the model to refine and abstract patterns across time steps.</li></ul><p>By learning from SPY’s historical log returns, the LSTM uncovers temporal patterns essential for predicting future returns. This approach is particularly effective for financial time series forecasting, where both short-term and long-term dependencies play critical roles.</p><h3>Data and Features</h3><h3>Data Used</h3><p>We use the monthly dataset for the LSTM model, as the daily and weekly datasets are too large and computationally expensive for the available resources. For some context, our PCs had insufficient memory and our IDEs crashed during training on the larger datasets.</p><p>The monthly dataset is suitable for forecasting long-term trends because the reduced dataset size significantly lowers computational requirements, making it feasible to train the LSTM effectively without compromising on the quality of long-term trend predictions.</p><h3>Feature Selected</h3><p>The main feature for the LSTM model is the <strong>log returns</strong> of SPY, as they align with the following considerations:</p><ul><li><strong>Magnitude Effects</strong>: Large and varying magnitudes in price data can lead to <strong>exploding gradients</strong> during LSTM training. High price values amplify gradients during backpropagation, and this effect compounds over multiple time steps, destabilising the model and preventing convergence. While scaling can mitigate this, raw prices still obscure relative changes. Using log returns avoids these issues by providing scaled, normalised inputs, reducing the risk of exploding gradients.</li><li><strong>Scaling :</strong> All <strong>l</strong>og returns are between [-1,1] which is very suitable for LSTM; we verified this during EDA. The data can be utilised directly without additional changes.</li></ul><h3>Training, Validation and Inference</h3><p>We split our data into training and test sets in temporal order in a 80% for training and 20% for testing/inference.</p><h3>Training the Model</h3><p>We run the LSTM Model over the entire dataset to allow it to learn the data. This run is known as an epoch. We have 550 epochs and a learning rate of 0.001. These hyperparameters have shown to give the most reasonable results after some manual tuning.</p><p>The training process involves the following steps for each epoch:</p><ol><li><strong>Forward Pass</strong>: Each batch of overlapping sequences is passed through the two LSTM layers to generate predictions for the target values.</li><li><strong>Loss Calculation</strong>: The predicted values are compared with the actual target values using a loss function, in our case, MSE.</li><li><strong>Backward Pass</strong>: Gradients are computed using Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) to update the model’s weights based on the calculated loss.</li><li>This process is repeated for multiple epochs (runs over the entire dataset) until the model learns to minimise the prediction error.</li></ol><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-4-prophet-and-lstm/HMS75HZ6c4q0YKsATUk0SQ.png" /><figcaption>Figure 17: Visualising the training lower being reduced as the number of epochs increases.</figcaption></figure><h3>Validation and Inference</h3><p>Validation was not performed for the LSTM due to the small dataset size and the high computational cost of training the model. With only 373 data points and a sequence length of 120, splitting the data further for validation would significantly reduce the training set, limiting the model’s ability to learn meaningful patterns.</p><p>For inference, the results are promising. It yielded an extremely low loss of 0.0022.</p><h3>Forecasting</h3><p>In traditional forecasting, a model generates a single deterministic prediction for each timestep, assuming that the system’s behaviour is entirely fixed. However, the future is uncertain and we want our results to reflect this.</p><p>The goal of this process is to provide a <strong>probabilistic forecast</strong> of log returns by estimating both the most likely trajectory (mean forecast) and the range of possible outcomes (confidence intervals). This is achieved using <strong>stochasticity</strong> through <a href="https://medium.com/@ciaranbench/monte-carlo-dropout-a-practical-guide-4b4dc18014b5">Monte Carlo Dropout</a> (MCD), a method that incorporates randomness into the model during prediction to simulate multiple plausible scenarios for the future.</p><p>To address this, stochasticity is introduced into the forecasting process. In a normal Neural Network, MCD shuts off some neurons to change the values of predictions. For LSTM, some outputs in each hidden layer is not passed on to the next, enabling stochasticity. This will then give us a distribution of predictions.</p><p>We can utilise this distribution to obtain a mean forecast and a confidence interval. To ensure that the results are reproducible, we run MCD with a seed of 42.</p><h3>Checking Normality of Data</h3><p>For a Z value to be used, the distribution needs to be normal. We flatten (combine all simulation outputs in each Tensor dimension into a single array), allowing the <strong>KDE plot</strong> to treat all values as part of one continuous distribution.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-4-prophet-and-lstm/gqea7Ujrzxq788jEv0TYwg.png" /><figcaption>Figure 18: Verifying that the distribution of log returns is normal by comparing the KDE plot of our predictions against a normal distribution curve.</figcaption></figure><p>The KDE plot resembles the Normal Distribution very closely. We can then use Z values.</p><h3>How Confidence Intervals are Calculated</h3><ol><li><strong>Distribution of Predictions</strong>: Multiple simulations generate a distribution of predicted values for each timestep.</li><li><strong>Mean Prediction</strong>: The average of these predictions represents the most likely value (mean forecast — $\mu$).</li><li><strong>Uncertainty</strong>: The standard deviation ($\sigma$) captures the variability of the predictions.</li><li><strong>Confidence Intervals</strong>: 1.96 is the Z-value for 95% confidence for our normally distributed predictions.</li></ol>

$$
\text{Lower Bound} = \mu - 1.96\sigma,
\qquad
\text{Upper Bound} = \mu + 1.96\sigma
$$

<p>After the COVID-19 pandemic, global inflation surged due to supply chain disruptions and labour shortages, causing sharp increases in the prices of goods, energy, and housing. By 2023, the rising cost of living led central banks like the U.S. Federal Reserve to raise interest rates to control inflation.</p><p>People often underestimate inflation’s long-term impact on their finances. While immediate price hikes are noticeable, the gradual erosion of purchasing power is less apparent.</p><p>Our short-term focus leads us to overlook inflation. For example, earning a 5% return when inflation is 3% yields a real return of only 2%, which can significantly affect wealth over decades. Recent inflation spikes have highlighted its true impact, often too late to prevent financial harm.</p><p>Part 2 of the project analyses SPY’s performance over time, comparing its returns to inflation rates to determine its effectiveness in preserving purchasing power during economic uncertainty.</p><h3>What is inflation?</h3><p>Inflation refers to the general increase in the price level of goods and services over time, which reduces the purchasing power of money.</p><p>As inflation rises, each unit of currency buys fewer goods and services. This erosion in value impacts everything from daily expenses to long-term investments, making it critical for individuals to account for inflation when planning their financial futures.</p><p>We will use the Consumer Price Index (CPI) as a measure of inflation in our analysis. It tracks changes in the prices of a fixed basket of goods and services commonly purchased by households. This basket typically includes items such as food, housing, clothing, transportation, medical care, and recreational services.</p><p>We selected CPI as it reflects the overall cost of living and is the most tangible inflation measure for consumer since it impacts their budgets and spending power.</p><h3>Assumptions</h3><p>The assumptions remain <a href="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-1-consistent-returns">consistent with those in Part 1</a>, with the exception of removing the assumption about inflation.</p><p>FRED data is only available up to 1st October 2024, so the values here will be less than that of Part 1 which goes up to December.</p><h3>The Economist’s Perception of Inflation</h3><p>My perspective of inflation comes from how households maximise their consumption across multiple time periods in Macroeconomics classes.</p><p>Although this model is very simplified, it still captures the impact of inflation on the saving habits of consumers. If you want to see what a more complex model is like, please refer to this paper : <a href="https://economics.smu.edu.sg/sites/economics.smu.edu.sg/files/economics/pdf/Seminar/2024/20240318.pdf">Optimal Monetary Policy during a Cost-of-Living Crisis by Olivi, A., Sterk, V., &amp; Xhani, D. (2024)</a>.</p><h4>Formulating the problem</h4><p>Imagine you have a certain amount of money to last your whole life — everything you’ll ever earn from your job or any savings. The <strong>lifetime budget constraint</strong> is like your financial plan for making sure that this money can <strong>maximise your intertemporal utility function</strong>, i.e. ****can covering all your needs, from now until retirement.</p><p>The key challenge is balancing how much you spend today versus how much you save for later. If you spend too much now, you might not have enough for future expenses. But if you save wisely, you can keep your spending steady, even when you’re no longer working.</p><p>This idea helps us understand how we should plan our finances over the long term, ensuring we don’t run out of money before we need it most.</p><p>Mathematically, the problem is:</p>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_{c_0,c_1}\quad &U(c_0,c_1) = \ln(c_0) + \beta \ln(c_1) \\
\text{s.t.}\quad &c_0(1+r) + c_1 = y_0(1+r) + y_1
\end{aligned}
$$

<p>This simple 2 period model should be familiar to anyone who has studied quantitative economics at an undergraduate level. It models how a household allocates resources between different time periods.</p><ul><li>Intertemporal Utility Function $U(c_0,c_1) = \ln(c_0) + \beta \ln(c_1)$: This equation represents your ‘satisfaction’ for consumption in 2 time periods, today (period 0) and the future (period 1). It is the sum of your utility in period 1 and period 2.<br>Also, $c_0,c_1,y_0,y_1 \in [0,\infty)$.<br>The parameter $\beta$, typically between 0 and 1, $\beta \in (0,1)$, represents impatience — people tend to value present consumption more than future consumption. A lower $\beta$ indicates greater impatience, giving less weight to future consumption satisfaction.</li><li>Lifetime budget constraint (LBC): $c_0(1+r) + c_1 = y_0(1+r) + y_1$. <strong>This equation represents all of the money and assets you will have in your entire life.<br></strong>This LBC shows that total lifetime consumption (LHS) cannot exceed total lifetime resources (RHS), meaning you must balance how much you spend now versus how much you save for future consumption. In essence, it’s the sum of all income and wealth available for consumption across your life.</li></ul><p>We will not be using constrained optimisation to solve for the optimal $c_0$ and $c_1$; instead, we look at how inflation and investing can impact our LBC.</p><h4>Considering Inflation</h4><p>One of the problem sets in the Macroeconomics classes presented an extremely fascinating case where an inflation term $1+\delta$, $\delta \in (0,1)$, is introduced. This changes the lifetime budget constraint:</p>

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_0(1+\delta)(1+r) + c_1(1+\delta) &= y_0(1+\delta)(1+r) + y_1 \\
c_0 + \frac{c_1}{1+r} &= y_0 + \frac{y_1}{(1+r)(1+\delta)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<p>Now that inflation is present in the model, there are several important points to take note from the equation. Suppose inflation at 2% outpaces the savings rate of the bank 1.5%, $\delta = 0.02$, $r = 0.015$, and $\delta \gt r$.</p><ul><li>The opportunity cost of not consuming more today increases.</li><li>The amount of resources needed to maintain the same level of consumption in the next period $c_1$ increases.</li><li>More importantly, <strong>you have less money and assets in your entire life because of inflation</strong></li></ul><p>If period 1 is your working period and period 2 is your retirement period, inflation effectively erodes the value of your retirement fund.</p><h3>Section 1 : Analysis on Inflation Rates</h3><p>We will use the SPY prices taken from Yahoo Finance and CPI data from FRED. A detailed explanation of the data is found in the appendix. Essentially, after pre-processing the data, we get a table of SPY adjusted inflation prices ordered by date in an ascending sequence.</p><p>Note that the base date for our CPI price is on 1 January 2000.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*icjjXKK4--qOAbFNT8Qjlg.png" /><figcaption>Figure 1 : Comparison between real and nominal values of SPY</figcaption></figure><p>The findings are quite astonishing. Initially, the difference between nominal and real prices was minimal, but over time, the divergence becomes significant. We can consider inflation as negative compounding, counteracting the positive compounding we observed earlier.</p><p>We will now proceed to examine how this impacts households.</p><p>Firstly, we observe the real returns of leaving US$ 10,000 in a savings account that yields 1.5% per annum.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*XQh-bhjAwW2YRwvyqH5G9Q.png" /><figcaption>Figure 2 : Comparison between real and nominal values of US $ 10,000 in a savings account</figcaption></figure><p>Although the investment grows nominally over time due to compounding interest, inflation significantly eroded its real value. In contrast, SPY still delivers a substantial positive return, even after accounting for inflation.</p><h3>Analysis on Real Returns — Lump Sum &amp; DCA</h3><p>To drive the point home, we will now look at the same case analysed in Part 1.1. The growth rate of SPY effectively outpaces the inflation rate.</li></ul><h3>Conclusion</h3><p>The analysis of SPY’s performance over time reveals its effectiveness as a hedge against inflation, particularly during periods of economic uncertainty. While inflation erodes purchasing power and presents significant challenges to wealth preservation, consistent investing in SPY has demonstrated the potential to outpace rising prices over the long term.</p><p>However, the study highlights that inflation-conscious financial planning is essential. Without considering inflation-adjusted returns, individuals risk underestimating the true impact on their wealth. The sharp spikes in inflation following the COVID-19 pandemic serve as a reminder of the importance of safeguarding investments against such disruptions.</p><p>By focusing on assets that historically perform well during inflationary periods, such as SPY, and adopting disciplined, long-term investment strategies, individuals can better preserve and grow their wealth. Ultimately, inflation-aware investing is not just a response to economic volatility but a critical approach to securing financial stability and purchasing power for the future.</p><p>But now you may ask, what if I have not started yet?

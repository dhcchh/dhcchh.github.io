By analysing historical trends and incorporating advanced predictive models, we will provide insights into the long-term growth prospects of SPY, offering a data-driven perspective on why starting today could still lead to significant financial growth in the future.</p><p>This part and the next are quite technical. If you are just interested in the results, feel free to skip to the <a href="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-4-prophet-and-lstm#final-results-and-conclusions">final results in Part 4</a>.</p><p>Predicting future prices or returns of SPY using machine learning models serves as a <strong>gauge of potential trends</strong>, not a definitive or robust prediction of actual prices or returns. Financial markets are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including macroeconomic conditions, geopolitical events, and investor sentiment, many of which cannot be fully captured in historical data.</p><p><strong>If accurately predicting future prices/returns were that easy, everyone would already be doing it, and the markets would quickly adjust, making it impossible to generate consistent profits from such predictions. As such, the results from this project should be interpreted as probabilistic scenarios rather than precise forecasts. While our models provide valuable insights into potential movements of SPY, it is not a substitute for comprehensive financial planning or professional investment advice.</strong></p><h3>Formulating the problem</h3><p>The goal of this part of the project is to forecast SPY’s future performance over the next 20 years using historical data, which depends on the modelling approach. The problem is framed as a time series forecasting task, where the challenge is to predict future values based on patterns in past data.</p><p>Given the sequential and temporal nature of financial data, the objective is to capture long-term dependencies while maintaining realistic and interpretable predictions. Recognizing the inherent uncertainty in financial forecasting, <strong>confidence intervals (CI)</strong> at a 95% level will be used wherever possible to provide a probabilistic range of outcomes rather than a single deterministic prediction. If producing a confidence interval is not feasible for a particular method, the primary forecast will be used instead.</p><h4>Evaluation Metrics</h4><p>We will use Mean Squared Error (MSE) to evaluate our models.</p>

$$
\operatorname{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{y}_i-y_i)^2
$$

<p>It is the average of the squared differences between the predicted and actual values, which penalises larger errors more heavily.</p><h3>Stacking Models</h3><p>To enhance the robustness and accuracy of the forecasts, the project incorporates multiple forecasting models. These models may predict future values such as prices or returns, depending on the specific use case. Each model captures different aspects of the data, such as short-term fluctuations, long-term trends, or recurring patterns in SPY’s historical performance.</p><p>The predictions from these models will be <strong>‘stacked’</strong>, using ensemble techniques to combine their outputs:</p><ul><li>Multiple models will independently forecast the target variable (e.g., prices or returns).</li><li>Their outputs will be aggregated by averaging to produce a final forecast.</li><li>This ensemble approach leverages the strengths of individual models, leading to more reliable predictions.</li></ul><p>By accommodating multiple forecasts, incorporating CIs, and stacking multiple models, the project ensures a comprehensive and robust approach to forecasting SPY’s long-term performance. This strategy balances interpretability, accuracy, and flexibility, providing actionable insights while accounting for uncertainties in financial markets.</p><h3>EDA &amp; Feature Engineering</h3><h3>Feature : Log Returns</h3><p>Log returns are used extensively as a feature for our models. They offer several advantages over raw prices for financial analysis and time series modelling. These are derived from the prices at neighbouring time steps. There are several important reasons for using this feature.</p><ol><li><strong>Scale Independence</strong>:<br>Log returns normalize the <strong>magnitude</strong> of price changes, ensuring that percentage changes are consistent across different price levels. Take these prices of SPY as an example:<br>On <strong>March 9, 2009</strong>, SPY’s closing price was <strong>$50.70</strong>, marking its lowest point during the 2008 financial crisis.<br>As of <strong>December 24, 2024</strong>, SPY’s price is approximately <strong>$594.69</strong>.<br>A <strong>$10 increase</strong> from these prices represents different proportional changes:<br>- <strong>$50.70</strong> to <strong>$60.70</strong> is a <strong>19.7%</strong> increase.<br>- <strong>$594.69</strong> to <strong>$604.69</strong> is a <strong>1.68%</strong> increase.<br>This illustrates that the same absolute change has a more significant impact when the initial price is lower.<br>Log returns capture these proportional changes, providing a consistent measure of relative performance over time.</li><li><strong>Simplified Multiplicative Relationships</strong>:<br>When working with log returns, the compounding effect of returns can be represented as a sum, making it computationally convenient for analysis and modelling. This is especially important when utilising computationally heavy models like Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), making it advantageous over simple returns.</li><li><strong>Stationarity</strong>:<br>Log returns tend to have more stable statistical properties (e.g., mean and variance) over time compared to raw prices, making them better suited for modelling and forecasting. We will show this later.</li></ol><h3>Calculating Log Returns</h3><p>Log returns are calculated from prices using the following formula:</p>

$$
r_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)
$$

<ul><li>$P_t$ is the price at time $t$.</li><li>$P_{t-1}$ is the price at the previous time step, $t-1$.</li><li>$r_t$ is the log return at time $t$.</li></ul><p>This formula gives the proportional change in prices on a logarithmic scale. It can be applied to all of the data at once to return the log returns column.</p><p>To reconstruct prices from log returns, the process is reversed:</p>

$$
P_t = P_{t-1} \times e^{r_t}
$$

<p>By applying this formula iteratively, prices can be computed from a series of log returns, starting from an initial price.</p><h3>Log Returns Plot</h3><p>First we visualise the plot of log returns of SPY from <strong>January 1993 to December 2024</strong>. This is a strong indicator of stationarity and we will prove it later.</li><li><strong>Volatility Clusters</strong>: Periods of high volatility are evident, where the amplitude of fluctuations increases, particularly around major financial events. For instance:</li></ol><ul><li>The spike in volatility around <strong>2008</strong> corresponds to the Global Financial Crisis.</li><li>Increased volatility is also visible during <strong>2020</strong>, which aligns with the COVID-19 pandemic.</li></ul><ol><li><strong>Symmetry</strong>: The returns exhibit symmetry, with both positive and negative spikes occurring over the time series, suggesting a balance of upward and downward movements.</li><li><strong>Persistence of Stability</strong>: Outside periods of economic crises, the log returns appear relatively stable, indicating lower volatility.</li></ol><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/iHr-TNbsv8IJJuTDfC1CmA.png" /><figcaption>Figure 1 : Pattern of Log Returns from January 1993 to December 2024</figcaption></figure><h3>Histogram of Log Returns</h3><p>The histogram has a log scale applied to the y-axis for easier visualisation of extreme difference in the number of log return values between extreme and less extreme categories. It is not plotted to scale.</p><p><strong>From the histogram of SPY log returns</strong> from <strong>January 1993 to December 2024, </strong>the following observations can be made:</p><ol><li><strong>Bell-Shaped Distribution</strong>:</li><li>The histogram somewhat resembles a <strong>normal distribution after log scaling the y axis</strong>, with most log returns concentrated around 0. This indicates that small daily fluctuations (close to no change in price) are the most common.</li><li><strong>Symmetry</strong>:<br>The distribution is approximately symmetric, with positive and negative returns occurring with similar frequency. These outliers often correspond to market shocks or significant economic events.</li></ol><p>We gain a statistical perspective on the behaviour of SPY’s log returns, showcasing its tendency toward stability while acknowledging the presence of rare but impactful extreme movements.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/eR3LMh2ouxFLuohWkQh5ug.png" /><figcaption>Figure 2: Histogram of all Log Returns from January 1993 to December 2024. The y-axis is log scaled for easier visualisation.</figcaption></figure><h3>Proving Stationarity in features</h3><p>Stationarity is a key assumption in time series analysis, where the statistical properties — mean, variance, and autocorrelation — are constant over time. To formally assess stationarity, we use the <strong>Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test</strong>.</p><p>$H_0$ : The time series has a unit root (i.e., it is non-stationary).</p><p>$H_1$ : The time series does not have a unit root (i.e., it is stationary).</p><h4>Log return results:</h4><p>We get: <br>ADF statistic: -16.5937; p-value: 0.0000</p><p>At the 99% significance level ($\alpha$ = 0.01), we reject the null hypothesis of the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, indicating that the log returns time series is stationary and does not require differencing.</p><h4>Close Price Results</h4><p>Close Price is not stationary based on these results:</p><p>ADF statistic: 4.3116; p-value: 1.0000</p><p>At the 99% significance level ($\alpha$ = 0.01), we do not reject the null hypothesis of the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, as there is insufficient evidence to disprove $H_0$.</p><h3>Truncated Data</h3><p>In this project, the original daily SPY data was aggregated into weekly and monthly datasets to manage computational constraints while preserving key information. While the truncated data suffices for identifying trends and training models, we will prioritise using the full dataset whenever computational resources allow to ensure the most comprehensive analysis.</p><h3>Naïve Benchmarks</h3><p>Before using more advanced models, we aim to establish a baseline prediction that models must come close to. To begin, let’s review the trend of SPY from its inception to the present.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/KUjZUqbwu7hG4s3q3lZsPA.png" /><figcaption>Figure 6: Visualisation of SPY’s adjusted close price from January 1993 to December 2024</figcaption></figure><h4>Linear Regression</h4><p>We can approximate the pattern of SPY’s price using linear regression. The shape of SPY data resembles a polynomial function like $x^2$ + $x^3$ and an exponential function like $e^x$. We can approximate this function using a linear regression. We use the number of days elapsed (scaled a factor of 1/100 to control the function’s growth) as a feature to predict SPY’s price.</p>

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 X^2 + \beta_2 X^3 + \varepsilon \\
y &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 e^X + \varepsilon
\end{aligned}
$$

<p>We will utilise several polynomials and one exponential function — $x^2$ + $x^3$, $x^2$, $x^3$ and $e^x$ to model the behaviour of SPY. The metric we will consider is Mean Squared Error (MSE).</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/SH3i2ebUAvYMeOynf2UAHQ.png" /><figcaption>Figure 7.1 : $e^x$ and $x^2$ against SPY data</figcaption></figure><p>First, we try $x^2$ and $e^x$. By visual inspection, they all seem to fit SPY’s known pattern decently well. The best performing regression model is $x^2$ + $x^3$ with the lowest MSE of approximately 600.</p><p>Finally, we extend the models to predict the price of SPY in 20 years.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/ZiUgE6qMUYOpQGDl_bK6Rg.png" /></figure><p>These are the baseline results.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/dsbdxGFkJAaPMyUen7YCdA.png" /></figure><p>We get a conservative estimate from $x^2$ but $x^2$ + $x^3$ gives a more aggressive growth scenario. $x^3$ balances both to give an intermediate prediction range.</p><p>While it is currently impossible to definitively determine whether advanced models outperform the baseline over a 20-year horizon, their utility lies in providing predictions that are consistent with historical trends and fall within a reasonable neighbourhood of the baseline estimates. If the advanced models produce forecasts drastically different from these baselines without theoretical or data-driven justification, it may indicate overfitting or unrealistic assumptions. Therefore, the baseline results serve as a sanity check to validate the plausibility of predictions from more complex models.</p><h3>Basic Returns Benchmark</h3><p>Another way to benchmark our results is to assume that the total return over the next 20 years will mirror the total return from the previous 20 years. While this assumption may not perfectly reflect future market behaviour, it provides a practical and straightforward basis for comparison.</p><p>The 20 Year Total Return for SPY is 611.35% (Source: <a href="https://www.financecharts.com/etfs/SPY/performance/total-return">https://www.financecharts.com/etfs/SPY/performance/total-return</a>, as of 24 Dec 2024). Another way of benchmarking is to check if the return has increased approximately 6 times.</p><p>For instance, the price of SPY on December 12 2024 is US$606.79, assuming the growth rate is the same, the price of SPY on December 12 2044 will reach US$3709.</p><p>Making reference to our Linear Regression model, this will put the $x^3$ model closest to the returns benchmark.</p><h3>Validating Our Models</h3><p>In this project, validation is crucial for assessing how well the model generalizes to unseen data when forecasting SPY’s future performance. Whenever possible, we will implement it. Unlike traditional machine learning problems, the sequential nature of time series data requires validation methods that preserve temporal order to avoid data leakage. We used expanding window cross-validation to provide a realistic evaluation by ensuring the model is trained only on past data and tested on future data, aligning with the real-world forecasting scenario.</p><h3>Forward Chaining/Expanding Window Cross Validation</h3><p>We choose this approach as we want our models to capture as much of the data as possible.</p><p>In forward chaining, the dataset is split into multiple training and validation sets while maintaining the chronological order. The key idea is to incrementally increase the size of the training set, using earlier observations to predict later ones. The validation set for each iteration always consists of data points that come chronologically after the training set.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/hi5pOXXqmYn3WVPUQPZcSA.png" /><figcaption>Figure 9: Forward Chaining/Expanding Window Cross Validation. Credits: <a href="https://medium.com/@pradip.samuel/cross-validation-in-time-series-model-b07fbba65db7">https://medium.com/@pradip.samuel/cross-validation-in-time-series-model-b07fbba65db7</a></figcaption></figure><h3>Validation Approach for This Project</h3><p>In this project, the dataset is first split into approximately <strong>80% training data</strong> and <strong>20% validation data</strong> to preserve a portion of the data for final evaluation. This may differ based on the model. Within the <strong>training set</strong>, validation is performed to assess the model’s performance before applying it to the validation set. This ensures that the model is both well-trained and generalises effectively without using future information from the <strong>validation</strong> set.</p><h3>How Validation is Performed on the Training Set</h3><p>We incrementally increase the size of the training set after validating.</p><ul><li><strong>Iteration 1</strong>: Train on $t_1$ to $t_{50}$, validate on $t_{51}$ to $t_{60}$.</li><li><strong>Iteration 2</strong>: Train on $t_1$ to $t_{60}$, validate on $t_{61}$ to $t_{70}$.</li><li><strong>Iteration 3</strong>: Train on $t_1$ to $t_{70}$, validate on $t_{71}$ to $t_{80}$.</li></ul><p>This process continues until all data points in the training set have been included in the test set at least once.</p><p>This approach balances the need for robust validation with the constraints of preserving temporal order, ensuring that the model is evaluated realistically while maintaining an unbiased test set for final performance assessment.</p><h3>Model — Monte Carlo</h3><p>Monte Carlo is a method that relies on many simulations. It involves sampling data randomly and then inferring the results from that data.</p><p>In this project, we perform a Monte Carlo simulation by sampling a distribution built from the past log returns and using that value as the current day’s log return. This is then done repeatedly to simulate future prices, as well as perform multiple simulations.</p><p>We used the full log returns dataset, as the time complexity of the simulation is unaffected by the size of the dataset. The feature used was log return, since the return does not account for the current value of the stock.</p><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/Sv1sU7_D7IFltztLLnEiAg.png" /><figcaption>Figure 10.1 : Histogram of the log returns</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="" src="/blog/spy-analytics-forecasting-part-3-forecasting-future-returns/gnIVV4-lk2cvs-u688uPCg.png" /><figcaption>Figure 10.2: Histogram of the log returns in each time period</figcaption></figure><p>As data and trends may become outdated, tests were performed to determine the cut-off for the earliest data that should be included. After splitting the data into 6 time periods, each containing 1336 days, a t-test was performed with the following hypotheses:</p><p>$H_0$ : Each split of the data has the same mean as the full dataset.</p><p>$H_1$ : Each split of the data has a different mean compared to the full dataset.</p><p>The respective p-values were 0.25,0.25, 0.20, 0.46, 0.95 and 0.58. At a 5% level of significance, none of the null hypotheses are rejected, and we can consider the entire dataset together as a single distribution without needing to remove earlier data from the dataset being used. In fact, We do expect periods with high growth, as well as periods with a decline, and having more data will allow us to have a better distribution to sample from.</p><h3>Simulation and Results</h3><p>Since the histograms show a shape that looks like a normal distribution, we will sample the log returns from a normal distribution with mean and variance equal to that of the data.
